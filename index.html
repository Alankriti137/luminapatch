The Problem

Medicine watches episodically.
Cancer grows continuously.

The single biggest preventable cause of late-stage breast cancer diagnoses is not a lack of treatment options, not a lack of awareness, and not a lack of technology. It is time. The time between a patient's last clear scan and the moment a growing tumour is finally detected. That window — often 6 months to a year — is where lives are lost.

In plain language
Imagine your car only has its engine checked twice a year. Between those checks, anything could go wrong — and you would have no way of knowing until it breaks down completely. That is exactly how breast cancer monitoring works today for even the highest-risk patients in the world. The checks happen. But the gaps between them are enormous. And cancer does not wait for appointments.

BRCA-positive patients and breast cancer survivors have no continuous monitoring between clinical appointments. All current surveillance is entirely episodic — a mammogram once a year, an MRI every 6 months at best. Between those appointments, breast tissue is completely unwatched.

If a tumour begins forming the day after a clear MRI, it will not be detected for up to 6 months. During that window it can progress from Stage 1 — where the 5-year survival rate exceeds 99% — to Stage 2 or Stage 3, where outcomes are significantly and measurably worse. A 2026 retrospective cohort study of 722 BRCA mutation carriers confirmed that even with intensive alternating 6-monthly MRI and mammography, interval cancers — tumours developing and growing in the gap between scheduled scans — still occurred. The surveillance gap is not a theoretical concern. It is a documented clinical reality.

The gap is not caused by a failure of clinical care. Doctors are doing everything currently available to them. The gap is caused by the absence of a tool that watches continuously — and that tool has never existed. Until now.

Status Quo

What exists today — and why it falls short.

Every current breast cancer monitoring tool shares the same fundamental limitation: it produces a single snapshot at a specific moment in time, then leaves the patient completely unmonitored until the next appointment. None of them watch. They look.

📷
Mammography
The standard screening tool for most women globally. Uses low-dose X-ray imaging to detect tumour masses. A radiologist reads the resulting image and identifies areas of concern. Annual mammography has meaningfully reduced breast cancer mortality in screened populations — but it has hard limits that technology cannot fully overcome. It produces a radiographic density map. It cannot measure blood vessel growth, tissue electrical properties, or molecular activity. It produces one data point per year. And it misses 15–20% of cancers in average density tissue, rising to 50% in dense tissue. Radiation exposure accumulates with every scan — a concern for patients who will need decades of monitoring.
1 data point per year
🧲
MRI
More sensitive than mammography and the current gold standard for BRCA+ surveillance. Uses magnetic fields and contrast dye to produce detailed soft tissue images. It can detect smaller tumours than mammography and is not limited by tissue density. For the highest-risk patients, MRI every 6 months alongside annual mammography is the recommended protocol. But MRI costs $1,000–$3,000 per scan, requires an intravenous contrast injection, takes 45–60 minutes in a confined scanner, and produces only 2 data points per year. The 6-month gap between MRI sessions remains a clinically documented vulnerability. For women with claustrophobia or contrast allergies, access is further limited.
2 data points per year
🖐️
Self-Examination
Breast self-examination involves the patient or a clinician feeling the breast tissue for lumps or changes. It has a role in breast cancer awareness — but as a detection tool, it has severe limitations. It can only detect lumps large enough to feel — typically at least 1–2cm in diameter, corresponding to a tumour that has already been growing for months to years. By the time a lump is palpable, the cancer is rarely at Stage 1. Self-examination produces no quantitative data, has no molecular or vascular sensitivity, and is highly variable depending on the individual's technique and anatomy. Clinical breast examination by a trained professional improves sensitivity but still faces the same fundamental limitation.

The common limitation
All current tools are reactive and periodic.
Mammography, MRI, and self-examination share a fundamental design limitation: they all require a patient to come to a tool at a scheduled moment in time. Between those moments, there is no monitoring. No continuous data. No early warning. The tool does not come to the patient. It does not watch. It looks once, produces a result, and then stops. For a disease that grows continuously and whose outcomes are so strongly determined by timing, this episodic approach leaves enormous preventable risk unaddressed.
What is missing
A tool that watches every day — not just twice a year.
What does not exist anywhere in the world today is a non-invasive, wearable, continuous breast tissue monitoring device. A tool that generates data every day. A tool that learns each patient's individual tissue baseline. A tool that detects the biological changes that precede visible tumour formation — haemoglobin shifts from early blood vessel growth, tissue stiffness changes from early fibrosis, temperature elevation from increased metabolic activity — and alerts the patient when those changes are sustained and meaningful. LuminaPatch is that tool.
The Solution

A wearable patch that watches your tissue every single day.

LuminaPatch uses four completely independent sensing channels simultaneously. A personalised AI model learns each patient's individual tissue signature over two weeks, then monitors continuously for multi-channel shifts consistent with early tumour development. All four channels must agree before any alert is generated — keeping false positives low while maintaining sensitivity to genuine biological change.

In plain language — what the patch actually does
The patch sits on your chest like a plaster. It is flexible, thin, and skin-coloured. It does not hurt, it does not require any needles, and it does not emit any harmful radiation. Inside the patch, four completely different types of sensors are reading your breast tissue simultaneously — using light, tiny electrical signals, temperature, and biological chemistry. Each of these four sensors is looking at a completely different physical property of your tissue. A personalised computer model running on the patch learns what your tissue normally looks like over two weeks, then watches every day for changes. If all four sensors change together in a pattern that looks like early cancer activity — and that change is sustained for at least three days — the patch sends a notification to your phone suggesting you contact your doctor sooner than your next scheduled scan.
💡
NIR Spectroscopy
In plain language: shines invisible light through your skin and reads what comes back.
Near-infrared light in the 700–900nm wavelength range passes through skin, fat, and breast tissue — reaching depths of up to 8cm — and the scattered light returning to detectors on the patch surface carries information about the tissue it passed through. Different tissue components absorb light differently: oxyhaemoglobin, deoxyhaemoglobin, water, and lipids each have distinct absorption signatures. Early tumours develop abnormal blood vessel networks (angiogenesis) to feed their growth — this increases total haemoglobin concentration in the affected area in a way NIR light can detect. Early fibrosis — tissue stiffening from tumour activity — changes how light scatters through the tissue. A 2025 study from Simon Fraser University and BC Cancer demonstrated 94.7% sensitivity and 84.2% specificity distinguishing healthy from malignant breast tissue using NIR diffuse optical tomography.
Source: Pouriayevali M. et al. Sensors, MDPI, February 2025. DOI: 10.3390/s25030983
⚡
Bioimpedance
In plain language: passes a tiny, completely safe electrical current through tissue and reads how it responds.
A low-amplitude alternating current — less than 1 milliampere, well below the threshold for any sensation — is passed between electrode pairs on the patch surface at multiple frequencies. The way tissue responds to this current — its impedance — reveals information about the tissue's structure. Healthy tissue and cancerous tissue respond differently because cancer cells have altered membrane structure, higher water content, and disrupted extracellular matrix architecture. These differences produce measurable shifts in the impedance spectrum that the patch reads continuously. A 2018 IEEE-published integrated device combined NIR spectroscopy and bioimpedance in a single wearable breast monitoring unit — validating that these two modalities together can differentiate healthy from cancerous breast tissue.
Source: IEEE Conference on Biomedical Engineering, 2018. DOI: 10.1109/ICBME.2018.8410543
🌡️
Thermal Sensing
In plain language: reads the temperature of your tissue continuously — tumours run warmer.
Developing tumours have two properties that elevate local tissue temperature: increased metabolic activity from rapidly dividing cells, and abnormal blood vessel networks that bring more blood flow to the area. Research has shown that malignant breast lesions run approximately 0.5–2°C warmer than surrounding healthy tissue. The patch contains a distributed array of miniaturised thermistors — passive temperature sensors that require almost no power to operate — which map the spatial temperature pattern across the tissue continuously. The AI layer looks for asymmetric or localised temperature elevations that are inconsistent with the patient's established personal baseline, accounting for normal temperature variation due to exercise, time of day, and environmental temperature.
Source: Medical thermography for breast cancer — multiple published clinical studies

Synthetic Biology Layer
In plain language: living engineered cells on the patch detect cancer molecules through the skin.
The innermost layer of the patch contains engineered microorganisms embedded in a biocompatible hydrogel matrix. These organisms carry a programmed genetic circuit: when specific cancer-associated volatile organic compounds — metabolic byproducts produced by cancer cells and secreted through the skin — reach a threshold concentration at the patch surface, the organisms activate a reporter gene that produces a measurable electrochemical signal. This signal feeds into the AI layer as a fourth completely independent detection channel. This modality is currently the most experimental of the four and is positioned as an additional specificity layer rather than a primary detection channel. Its novel contribution is molecular-level biological sensing that electronic sensors alone cannot replicate.

Personalised AI — not population averages.
Every existing breast cancer monitoring tool compares a patient to a population average — what an average woman's breast tissue looks like on a mammogram, what an average haemoglobin concentration looks like in breast tissue. But patients are not averages. A change that is completely normal for one woman — driven by her menstrual cycle phase, her hydration level, her age-related tissue density — could be highly abnormal for another. LuminaPatch compares each patient to herself. Her own biological baseline, established over two weeks of continuous monitoring, becomes the reference point for everything that follows. This is the only way to detect the subtle, individual-specific changes that precede tumour formation in real patients — and it has never been done in a wearable continuous monitoring device before.

How It Works

From first application to clinical alert — step by step.

LuminaPatch is designed to integrate into a patient's life with minimal friction and maximal continuity. It is prescribed by an oncologist or genetic counsellor as part of an existing surveillance protocol — not as a replacement for clinical care, but as the continuous complement to it that has always been missing.

01
Receive & Apply
The patient receives LuminaPatch through their oncologist or genetic counsellor. The flexible patch — roughly credit card sized, approximately 1mm thick — is placed directly on the chest over the area of highest concern as identified by the clinical team. It adheres through gentle flexible contact with the skin, requiring no special adhesive, no medical procedure, and no clinic visit. It pairs with the patient's phone automatically via Bluetooth on first contact. The entire application process takes under two minutes.
02
Baseline Learning
Over the first 14 days, the patch operates in learning mode. All four sensing channels collect data continuously, building a comprehensive picture of what the patient's tissue normally looks like across their individual biological variation — menstrual cycle phase, daily activity patterns, hydration levels, sleep, and environmental temperature. The app shows the patient their data building up day by day. This personalisation period is critical: without it, the system cannot distinguish meaningful change from normal variation. No alerts are generated during this period.
03
Continuous Monitoring
From day 15 onward, the patch monitors continuously in the background while the patient goes about their normal life. The patient wears it under clothing during the day and while sleeping. The app runs silently, logging all four channels continuously. Patients can open the app at any time to see their personal data timeline, check the current status of each sensing channel, and add personal notes — symptoms, medication changes, stressful periods, lifestyle events — that provide context for the sensor readings. The patch never needs charging because it is powered by the patient's own breathing through a piezoelectric energy harvester.
04
Alert & Action
When the AI detects a sustained multi-channel signal shift — all four channels deviating from personal baseline simultaneously for 72 or more consecutive hours in a cancer-consistent pattern — the patient receives a calm, informative notification. It describes what was detected and over what timeframe. It does not diagnose. It recommends the patient review their data and consider contacting their doctor sooner than their next scheduled scan. The patient reviews their full data log, adds personal notes for context, and decides whether to generate a shareable clinical report. The doctor receives a structured report — not a vague alert — with quantitative data, trend analysis, and the patient's own annotations.
The App — What The Patient Sees
A personal data timeline the patient controls entirely.
The LuminaPatch app shows the patient a continuous timeline of their four sensing channels — displayed as visual trends rather than raw numbers, so the information is meaningful without requiring scientific background. A stable baseline looks flat. A sustained shift looks like a rising or falling trend. The patient can add notes to any point in their timeline — symptoms they noticed, medications they started, travel, exercise patterns. These annotations sit alongside the sensor data and give it personal context. When the patient chooses to share data with their doctor, they generate a structured report that includes both the sensor trends and their own notes. No data ever reaches a doctor without the patient actively choosing it.
Patient Control — What Never Happens Automatically
The patch observes. The patient decides.
LuminaPatch is designed around a fundamental principle of patient data sovereignty. The AI detects and logs. The patient reviews and decides. No data is sent to a doctor automatically. No action — including any optional therapeutic response features — happens without the patient actively choosing it. When the AI flags a sustained signal, the patient receives a notification. They open the app, review their personal data, add any context they want, and decide whether the pattern warrants sharing with their clinical team. This is not a liability limitation — it is a design philosophy. Patients adopt and trust devices that put them in control. Devices that act without consent do not get worn.
Wear Duration
Designed for continuous long-term wear.
LuminaPatch is designed to be worn continuously for months between clinical appointments — not just for daily testing sessions. The flexible substrate conforms to body movement during exercise, sleep, and daily activity. The patch is shower-resistant. The biocompatible materials are safe for prolonged skin contact. Power is harvested passively from chest wall movement during breathing, supplemented by a thin-film photovoltaic layer on the outer surface that harvests ambient light. The patient does not need to charge it, replace batteries, or remove it for daily activities.
False Positive Prevention
Why the system is designed to not over-alert.
The most important design challenge in any continuous monitoring device is false positives — alerts that are not clinically meaningful. A device that alerts too often gets removed and abandoned. LuminaPatch addresses this through three layers of specificity. First, personalised baseline comparison means the system is looking for changes relative to that specific patient's normal, not a population average. Second, four independent sensing channels must all shift simultaneously — each measuring a completely different physical property of the tissue. Third, the shift must be sustained for at least 72 consecutive hours. A single bad reading, a brief fluctuation from a sunburn or minor inflammation, or a normal menstrual cycle change will not trigger an alert. Only a sustained, multi-channel, biologically consistent pattern will.

A common question is whether it makes sense to wear a monitoring patch continuously — not just during a daily testing session. The answer is yes, for a specific and important reason: the value of LuminaPatch is not in detecting a tumour at a single point in time. Technical Depth

How it is built — technically and in plain language.

LuminaPatch integrates four validated sensing modalities on a single flexible substrate, managed by a low-power microcontroller running a personalised AI inference model on-device. The technical gap between current component capabilities and a fully functional integrated device is under five years. Every individual component technology has been independently validated in peer-reviewed research.

In plain language — what the electronics actually are
Think of it like a very sophisticated smartwatch — worn on the chest instead of the wrist.
A smartwatch uses sensors — accelerometer, heart rate monitor, GPS — to continuously collect data about your body. It has a processor that interprets that data. It connects to your phone to share information. LuminaPatch does the same thing, but with four sensors specifically designed to read the biological properties of breast tissue, and an AI model trained to detect the specific pattern of changes associated with early tumour development. The electronics are miniaturised versions of equipment that already exists in clinical research settings. The innovation is integration — assembling them onto a single flexible patch, combining them with a personalised AI model, and making the whole system wearable and continuous.
Technical overview
One flexible substrate. Four sensing modalities. One AI model. One microcontroller.
The patch is built on a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) flexible substrate — a biocompatible material used widely in wearable medical devices that conforms to skin contours. All electronic components are mounted using serpentine interconnects — a wiring architecture that allows the substrate to bend without breaking circuit connections, validated by the UNC ANIRP patch research in 2024. A single ESP32 microcontroller manages all sensing channels, runs the on-device AI inference, controls Bluetooth data transmission, and manages the optional therapeutic response features. Power comes from a thin-film piezoelectric generator harvesting chest wall movement during breathing, supplemented by a photovoltaic layer.
NIR Spectroscopy System
700–900nm multi-wavelength LED array with photodetector grid
Plain language: tiny light emitters and receivers that read your tissue chemistry through the skin.
Multiple LED wavelengths are selected to target the differential absorption signatures of oxyhaemoglobin, deoxyhaemoglobin, water, and lipids — the four primary tissue components whose concentrations change with early tumour development. Photodetectors at defined source-detector separations of 2–4cm — optimised for breast tissue penetration at relevant depths — capture the scattered returning light. The ratio of absorbed to scattered light at each wavelength encodes the concentrations of each tissue component. Changes in haemoglobin concentration and tissue scattering coefficient over time are the primary indicators the AI model monitors. A 2025 MRI-guided NIR system validated 3D breast tissue reconstruction using flexible optoelectronic strips with 2,304 source-detector positions across six wavelengths, confirming that NIR can localise and characterise breast tissue changes at clinically relevant resolutions.
LED wavelengths: 700–900nm, multi-wavelength array
Penetration depth: up to 8cm in breast tissue
Source-detector separation: 2–4cm optimised for breast
Validated sensitivity: 94.7% (SFU and BC Cancer, 2025)
Measurement cycle: configurable duty cycling for power management
Bioimpedance System
Multi-frequency AC sweep with tetrapolar electrode configuration
Plain language: passes a current too small to feel through the tissue and reads how it responds — cancer tissue responds differently.
A tetrapolar electrode configuration is used — two electrodes inject current while two separate electrodes measure the resulting voltage — minimising contact impedance artefacts that would otherwise contaminate the measurement. The alternating current sweeps from 1kHz to 1MHz in multiple steps, extracting the Cole-Cole parameters of the tissue at each frequency. The Cole-Cole model is a validated mathematical description of tissue electrical behaviour that changes characteristically with malignancy: cancerous tissue has altered membrane capacitance, higher intracellular water content, and disrupted extracellular matrix that shifts both the resistive and reactive components of tissue impedance in measurable ways. The AD5933 impedance analyser chip — commercially available, low-power, and compact enough for wearable integration — executes the frequency sweep and outputs the impedance spectrum for AI processing.
Current amplitude: <1mA — below any sensation threshold
Frequency sweep: 1kHz to 1MHz multi-step
Configuration: tetrapolar — minimises contact artefacts
Chip: AD5933 impedance analyser or equivalent
Output: Cole-Cole parameters for AI feature extraction
Thermal Sensing System
Distributed thermistor array with 0.1°C resolution
Plain language: an array of tiny thermometers spread across the patch that map temperature continuously — tumours run warmer.
A distributed array of miniaturised negative temperature coefficient (NTC) thermistors are embedded across the patch surface at a density of approximately one per square centimetre. Each thermistor independently measures local skin surface temperature with 0.1°C resolution — sufficient to detect the 0.5–2°C local temperature elevation documented in association with breast malignancy. The spatial distribution of the thermistor array allows the AI model to detect asymmetric or localised heat signatures — not just overall temperature changes, which would be confounded by exercise and environmental temperature. The pattern of temperature elevation relative to surrounding tissue, tracked against the patient's personal spatial baseline, is the meaningful signal. Thermistors are passive sensors requiring almost no power, making this the lowest-power sensing channel in the stack.
Resolution: 0.1°C — detects clinically relevant temperature differences
Array density: approximately 1 sensor per cm²
Sampling rate: continuous, one reading per second per element
Power requirement: minimal — passive NTC sensors
AI feature: spatial heat pattern, not absolute temperature
Synthetic Biology Layer
Engineered microorganisms in biocompatible hydrogel
Plain language: living engineered cells that detect cancer-specific chemical signals passing through the skin — like a biological alarm system built into the patch.
The skin-contact layer of the patch contains engineered microorganisms embedded in a polyethylene glycol (PEG) or alginate-based biocompatible hydrogel matrix. The chassis organisms — E. coli Nissle 1917 or Bacillus subtilis, both established as safe for skin-contact applications in research settings — carry a synthetic genetic circuit. The circuit consists of a VOC-responsive promoter linked to an electrochemical reporter gene: when cancer-associated volatile organic compounds reach threshold concentrations at the patch surface, the promoter activates and drives expression of the reporter, producing a measurable electrochemical signal detectable by an embedded electrode integrated into the hydrogel layer. An engineered kill switch — a genetic circuit that causes organism self-termination if environmental conditions outside the defined operational range are detected — provides biosafety containment by design. This modality is the most experimental and is positioned as an additional specificity channel rather than a primary detection system.
Chassis: E. coli Nissle 1917 or B. subtilis
Circuit: VOC-responsive promoter — electrochemical reporter
Containment: engineered kill switch genetic circuit
Matrix: PEG or alginate hydrogel
Role: fourth independent channel — experimental specificity layer
AI and Processing
LSTM personalised baseline model running on ESP32 microcontroller
Plain language: a small computer model running on the patch itself that learns your personal tissue pattern and detects meaningful change.
The on-device AI is a lightweight Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) neural network — a type of recurrent neural network well-suited to time-series data — optimised for deployment on the ESP32 microcontroller with minimal power consumption. During the 2-week baseline period, the model is trained on the patient's own data to establish their personal baseline across all four channels, accounting for known sources of variation such as menstrual cycle phase, time of day, and activity level. After baseline, the model runs inference continuously — comparing incoming sensor readings to the established personal baseline and computing an anomaly score for each channel. An alert is generated only when anomaly scores for all four channels simultaneously exceed their individual thresholds for a minimum of 72 consecutive hours. This convergence requirement — four independent channels, simultaneously, for three days — is the core false positive prevention mechanism and the key technical innovation that makes continuous wearable monitoring clinically viable.
Architecture: LSTM time-series anomaly detection
Hardware: ESP32 microcontroller with Bluetooth Low Energy
Alert threshold: 72-hour simultaneous four-channel convergence
Target sensitivity: detect signal shifts ≥4 weeks before imaging
Target specificity: false positive rate below 5%
Power System
Piezoelectric energy harvesting from breathing — no charging required
Plain language: the patch is powered by your own breathing. Your chest moves when you breathe, and that movement generates the electricity the patch needs to run.
The primary power source is a thin-film piezoelectric generator embedded in the patch substrate. Piezoelectric materials generate electrical voltage when mechanically deformed — and the chest wall moves continuously with every breath, providing a consistent mechanical energy input. This energy is converted to electrical current and stored in a supercapacitor, which powers the sensing circuits and microcontroller. A thin-film photovoltaic layer on the outer surface of the patch harvests ambient light during waking hours, supplementing the piezoelectric generation and ensuring the supercapacitor maintains adequate charge. Duty cycling — activating sensing circuits for a measurement window and returning to low-power sleep mode between measurements — manages power consumption to ensure continuous operation under normal daily activity conditions. The system is designed to never require charging and never require battery replacement for the duration of wear.
Primary: piezoelectric thin-film — chest wall movement during breathing
Secondary: thin-film photovoltaic — ambient light harvesting
Storage: supercapacitor — fast charge/discharge cycling
Management: duty cycling for continuous operation
Result: no charging, no battery replacement required

A retrospective cohort study of 722 female BRCA1 and BRCA2 mutation carriers enrolled between January 2007 and December 2024 confirmed that even with structured high-risk surveillance comprising biannual clinical breast examination and alternating 6-monthly MRI and mammography, interval cancers still occurred. 89.1% of detected cancers were diagnosed at Stage 0 or I — confirming that intensive surveillance dramatically improves outcomes — but also confirming that gaps between appointments represent a documented clinical vulnerability that continuous monitoring would directly address.
Evaluating high-risk breast cancer surveillance outcomes in BRCA mutation carriers. Breast Cancer, Springer Nature, February 2026. DOI: 10.1007/s12282-026-01822-x

Roadmap

From validation study to clinical reality.

LuminaPatch follows a staged development pathway designed to prove the highest-risk scientific questions first — using existing validated sensors and real patient data — before committing capital to hardware development. This approach minimises financial risk while maximising the scientific value of each stage.

Stage 1
Now — Year 1
AI Signal Validation Observational Study
A 6-month prospective observational study enrolling 50–100 BRCA+ patients and breast cancer survivors across two or three clinical sites. Each participant wears a research-grade combination of existing validated sensors — a clinical NIR spectroscopy probe, a bioimpedance measurement system, and a skin temperature array — during standardised daily 20-minute sessions at home. All sensor data is time-stamped alongside standard clinical surveillance results — MRI and mammogram as clinically scheduled. No new hardware is built for this stage. The study tests one question: do the multimodal signals shift detectably before imaging findings, and if so, how many weeks earlier? Kill condition: if signals do not shift detectably or if false positive rate is unacceptably high despite multimodal convergence, the fundamental sensing approach requires revision before further investment.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions answered.

Does LuminaPatch diagnose cancer?
No. LuminaPatch is a continuous monitoring device, not a diagnostic tool. It detects sustained changes in tissue signals that are consistent with early tumour development and alerts the patient to seek clinical evaluation sooner than their next scheduled appointment. All diagnosis happens through standard clinical pathways — imaging, biopsy, and pathology review by qualified clinicians. LuminaPatch's role is to close the gap between those clinical appointments by providing continuous data that triggers earlier evaluation — not to replace the evaluation itself.
How does it avoid false alarms?
Through three layers of specificity working together. First, personalised baseline comparison: the system compares each patient's readings to her own established baseline, not a population average — distinguishing changes that are normal for her from changes that are not. Second, four-channel convergence: all four independent sensing channels must shift simultaneously in a cancer-consistent direction. Each channel measures a completely different physical property of the tissue, and the probability of all four shifting together due to normal biological variation is very low. Third, 72-hour sustained duration: brief fluctuations, single-day anomalies, and temporary changes due to exercise, minor illness, or hormonal shifts do not trigger alerts. Only sustained, multi-channel, biologically consistent patterns do.
Is the electrical current in the bioimpedance channel safe?
Yes. The bioimpedance measurement uses a current amplitude of less than 1 milliampere — well below the threshold for any sensation (approximately 1mA) and thousands of times below any level associated with physiological harm. Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) devices, used in physiotherapy clinics since the 1970s with no serious adverse events, use the same fundamental principle at similar or higher current levels. The NIR light is non-ionising — it produces no radiation exposure, accumulates no biological damage, and is safe for continuous daily use indefinitely. LuminaPatch is designed to be significantly safer than mammography in terms of cumulative exposure risk.
Who owns the data the patch generates?
The patient owns their data entirely and unconditionally. All sensor readings are logged locally on the device and in the patient's app. No data is ever transmitted to a doctor, a clinical team, or LuminaPatch's servers without the patient actively choosing to generate and share a report. Patients can view their full data timeline at any time, add their own notes, and delete their data. When a patient chooses to share data with their doctor, they generate a structured clinical report from within the app — the doctor receives what the patient chose to send, not a live data feed. This model is not a legal limitation; it is a fundamental design philosophy. Patients adopt and continue using devices they trust, and trust requires control.
Does it work for all breast cancer types?
The patch detects changes in the biological properties of breast tissue that are common across most invasive breast cancer types — haemoglobin changes from angiogenesis, tissue stiffness from fibrosis, and temperature elevation from metabolic activity. These biological events occur broadly across invasive ductal carcinoma, invasive lobular carcinoma, and triple-negative breast cancers. Performance by specific cancer subtype, hormone receptor status, and HER2 status will be characterised during the Stage 1 validation study and the subsequent clinical trial. The system is not designed to type or classify the cancer — only to flag that biological changes consistent with early tumour development are occurring and warrant earlier clinical evaluation.
What stage is LuminaPatch at right now?
LuminaPatch is at the early concept and validation design stage. The scientific foundations for each individual sensing modality have been validated independently in peer-reviewed research. The integration concept — four modalities, personalised AI baseline, continuous wearable format — is novel and has not been built before. The immediate next step is a $1 million observational study to confirm that the multimodal signal pattern exists and is detectable in real patient tissue before committing capital to hardware development. We are actively seeking research partners at university biomedical engineering departments, clinical collaborators in oncology and genetic counselling, and early-stage investors with experience in medical device development timelines.
Why four sensing channels instead of just one?
Because no single sensing modality is specific enough to support continuous at-home monitoring without an unacceptable false positive rate. NIR signals change with hydration, hormonal cycles, and tissue density variation. Bioimpedance changes with skin moisture and electrode contact pressure. Thermal signals change with exercise and environmental temperature. Each individual channel has too many confounders to generate reliable alerts on its own. But each channel measures a completely different physical property of the tissue — and the biological events associated with early tumour development change all four simultaneously in a characteristic pattern. Requiring convergence across all four channels, for a sustained period, is what makes it possible to distinguish a genuine early cancer signal from the background noise of normal daily biological variation.
How does the patch stay powered?
The patch harvests energy from two passive sources that require nothing from the patient. The primary source is a thin-film piezoelectric generator that converts the mechanical movement of the chest wall during breathing into electrical energy stored in a supercapacitor. Every breath generates a tiny amount of electricity — and breathing is continuous, so power generation is continuous. A secondary thin-film photovoltaic layer on the outer surface harvests ambient light during waking hours, supplementing the piezoelectric generation. The sensing circuits use duty cycling — activating for a measurement window and returning to low-power sleep mode between measurements — to manage power consumption within the available harvested energy. The result is a patch that operates indefinitely without ever needing to be charged or have its battery replaced.
About LuminaPatch

Built on a simple conviction.

LuminaPatch was founded on the belief that the gap between clinical appointments is one of the most preventable causes of late-stage breast cancer diagnoses — and that the technology to close that gap already exists. Four independently validated sensing modalities. A personalised AI model. A wearable form factor. Nobody has assembled them into a continuous breast cancer surveillance system. We are doing exactly that, with rigorous scientific methodology and unconditional respect for patient data sovereignty.

Patient First
The patient owns their data. The patient controls what their doctor receives and when. No automatic data transmission. No surveillance without informed, active consent. Every design decision — from the alert threshold to the data sharing mechanism — is made with patient trust and autonomy as the primary constraint. A device that patients do not trust is a device that gets removed. Trust is not a nice-to-have feature. It is the most critical engineering requirement in the entire system.

We know what we have validated and what we have not yet validated. Equity-Driven
